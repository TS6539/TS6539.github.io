topics:
  - kind: "Talk"
    title: "Writing with AI without losing the learner"
    description: "When students can ask ChatGPT to draft, summarize and revise, what learning is still happening on their side? This talk draws on my study of Japanese first-year undergraduates writing source-based English arguments with ChatGPT, published in the ICQE 2026 proceedings. Using epistemic network analysis of their writing process, it shows which patterns of AI use went with stronger and weaker writing, and what that suggests for teaching and assessment."
    suited: "language teachers, writing program staff, and faculty development audiences."
  - kind: "Talk"
    title: "Designing generative AI guidelines for university language programs"
    description: "Most university AI policies are either too vague to guide a class or too strict to survive contact with students. This talk shares how I am developing generative AI guidelines for English education at Hiroshima University, starting from a review of guidelines across Japanese universities and pairing each principle with concrete examples of student AI use. Participants leave with a framework they can adapt to their own program."
    suited: "program coordinators, language centers, and faculty development sessions."
  - kind: "Workshop"
    title: "A practical introduction to quantitative ethnography and ENA"
    description: "Quantitative ethnography combines close qualitative reading with network models of how ideas connect in discourse. In this hands-on workshop, participants move from coded data to an epistemic network, learn to read and compare networks, and see how the method has been used in studies of AI use, reading identity and doctoral publishing. No programming background is needed."
    suited: "graduate students and education researchers working with interview, discussion or process data. Works as a 90-minute session or a half-day workshop."
  - kind: "Talk"
    title: "AI-supported systematic reviews, and what still needs a human"
    description: "AI tools can now screen studies and extract data at a scale no review team could manage by hand. Drawing on my work on an NIH-funded project building AI-supported review software, and on research into Google Scholar as a primary search database, this talk covers where AI genuinely speeds up evidence synthesis and which steps still need careful human judgment."
    suited: "researchers, librarians, and graduate methods courses."
